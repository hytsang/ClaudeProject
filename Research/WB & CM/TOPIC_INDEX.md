# Berkshire Hathaway Letters — Topic Index (1977–2024)

A topic-organized companion to the year-by-year [`INDEX.md`](INDEX.md). Each sub-topic carries a short summary of how Warren Buffett (and, where noted, Charlie Munger) treats the theme across the letters, followed by representative passages quoted verbatim with the year cited. File references like `letters/2009.txt` point to the source file in this folder.

All quotations below come from the official Berkshire Hathaway letter archive at <https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/letters.html>. Where formatting from PDF or HTML conversion produced line breaks inside a sentence, those have been silently joined for readability; the wording itself is unchanged.

## Section 1 — Investment philosophy & principles

### 1.1 Margin of safety

Buffett treats "margin of safety" as the cornerstone Graham handed him, and quotes it almost identically across four decades. He frames it first as a price-discipline test on individual securities (1992), later widens it to the cost of float and capital structure (1990), then in 2023 generalizes it to how regulated utilities are supposed to plan capacity.

> "Ben Graham forcefully rejected the dagger thesis: 'Confronted with a challenge to distill the secret of sound investment into three words, we venture the motto, Margin of Safety.' Forty-two years after reading that, I still think those are the right three words. The failure of investors to heed this simple message caused them staggering losses as the 1990s began."
> — 1990 letter

> "Second, and equally important, we insist on a margin of safety in our purchase price. If we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not interested in buying."
> — 1992 letter

> "Today's price levels, though, have materially eroded the 'margin of safety' that Ben Graham identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing."
> — 1997 letter

### 1.2 Intrinsic value (and "look-through" earnings)

Intrinsic value is the metric Buffett insists shareholders track, even though it cannot be calculated precisely. Book value is offered only as a "tracking device" (2009); the Owner's Manual (reproduced beginning 1996) lays out the definition. The "look-through earnings" concept — adding Berkshire's pro-rata share of investee earnings to its own operating earnings — debuts in the 1990 letter and is tabulated annually through 1997.

> "Additionally, book value at most companies understates intrinsic value, and that is certainly the case at Berkshire. In aggregate, our businesses are worth considerably more than the values at which they are carried on our books. … Even so, Charlie and I believe that our book value – understated though it is – supplies the most useful tracking device for changes in intrinsic value."
> — 2009 letter

> "Calculations of intrinsic value are far from precise. Consequently, neither of us feels any urgency to buy an estimated $1 of value for a very real 95 cents."
> — 2019 letter

> "I believe Berkshire's intrinsic value can be approximated by summing the values of our four asset-laden groves and then subtracting an appropriate amount for taxes eventually payable on the sale of marketable securities."
> — 2018 letter

> "The following table sets forth our 1997 look-through earnings, though I warn you that the figures can be no more than approximate, since they are based on a number of judgment calls."
> — 1997 letter

### 1.3 Mr. Market & market psychology

The 1987 letter contains the canonical statement of Graham's Mr. Market allegory. Buffett returns to it in 1993 and in scattered later years to dismiss beta and short-term price action.

> "Imagine market quotations as coming from a remarkably accommodating fellow named Mr. Market who is your partner in a private business. Without fail, Mr. Market appears daily and names a price at which he will either buy your interest or sell you his. … For, sad to say, the poor fellow has incurable emotional problems."
> — 1987 letter

> "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful. If he shows up some day in a particularly foolish mood, you are free to either ignore him or to take advantage of him, but it will be disastrous if you fall under his influence."
> — 1987 letter

> "The more manic-depressive this chap is, the greater the opportunities available to the investor. That's true because a wildly fluctuating market means that irrationally low prices will periodically be attached to solid businesses."
> — 1993 letter

### 1.4 Circle of competence

The phrase enters the corpus in the mid-1990s in the context of why Buffett would not own technology stocks, and is restated repeatedly through 2013 to justify both passes and selective buys.

> "The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital."
> — 1996 letter

> "If we have a strength, it is in recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of competence and when we are approaching the perimeter. Predicting the long-term economics of companies that operate in fast-changing industries is simply far beyond our perimeter."
> — 1999 letter

> "It's vital, however, that we recognize the perimeter of our 'circle of competence' and stay well inside of it. Even then, we will make some mistakes, both with stocks and businesses."
> — 2013 letter

### 1.5 Long-term horizon & "favorite holding period is forever"

The longest-running motto in the letters; the famous "favorite holding period is forever" line appears in the 1988 letter announcing the Coca-Cola purchase. Variants reappear constantly.

> "In 1988 we made major purchases of Federal Home Loan Mortgage Pfd. ('Freddie Mac') and Coca Cola. We expect to hold these securities for a long time. In fact, when we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever."
> — 1988 letter

### 1.6 Risk vs. volatility

The 1993 letter contains Buffett's frontal attack on beta as a definition of risk. The argument is that the *business* — not the price chart — defines risk, and that volatility is opportunity, not danger, for a long-term owner.

> "Academics … define investment 'risk' differently, averring that it is the relative volatility of a stock or portfolio of stocks. … Employing data bases and statistical skills, these academics compute with precision the 'beta' of a stock … In their hunger for a single statistic to measure risk, however, they forget a fundamental principle: It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong."
> — 1993 letter

> "For owners of a business — and that's the way we think of shareholders — the academics' definition of risk is far off the mark, so much so that it produces absurdities. For example, under beta-based theory, a stock that has dropped very sharply compared to the market — as had Washington Post when we bought it in 1973 — becomes 'riskier' at the lower price than it was at the higher price."
> — 1993 letter

### 1.7 Mistakes acknowledged

Buffett uses the letters to publicly catalog his errors. The signature treatment is the 1989 "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years (A Condensed Version)" section. He returns to mistakes recurringly: the Dexter Shoe acquisition (later admitted to be paid for with Berkshire stock that compounded the damage), the original Berkshire Hathaway textile purchase, and the 2024 farewell letter where he opens with mistakes acknowledged.

> "Robert Benchley: 'Having a dog teaches a boy fidelity, perseverance, and to turn around three times before lying down.' Such are the shortcomings of experience. Nevertheless, it's a good idea to review past mistakes before committing new ones. So let's take a quick look at the last 25 years."
> — 1989 letter, opening of "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years"

> "Finally, I made an even worse mistake when I said 'yes' to Dexter, a shoe business I bought in 1993 for $433 million in Berkshire stock (25,203 shares of A). What I had assessed as durable competitive advantage vanished within a few years. But that's just the beginning: By using Berkshire stock, I compounded this error hugely."
> — 2007 letter

> "Sometimes I've made mistakes in assessing the future economics of a business I've purchased for Berkshire – each a case of capital allocation gone wrong. That happens with both judgments about marketable equities – we view these as partial ownership of businesses – and the 100% acquisitions of companies."
> — 2024 letter

### 1.8 Mentors and reading list

Buffett repeatedly credits Ben Graham, Phil Fisher, Tom Murphy (Cap Cities), Katharine Graham (Washington Post), and — over decades — Charlie Munger. The 2023 letter is essentially an extended tribute to Munger; the 2024 letter explicitly names Tom Murphy.

> "Long ago, Ben Graham taught me that 'Price is what you pay; value is what you get.' Whether we're talking about socks or stocks, I like buying quality merchandise when it is marked down."
> — 2008 letter

> "Ben Graham said, 'Day to day, the stock market is a voting machine; in the long term it's a weighing machine.' If you keep making something more valuable, then some wise person is going to notice it and start buying."
> — 2022 letter

> "When discussing problems at specific subsidiaries, we do, however, try to follow the advice Tom Murphy gave to me 60 years ago: 'praise by name, criticize by category.'"
> — 2024 letter

## Section 2 — Business quality & "moats"

### 2.1 Economic moats / durable competitive advantage

Buffett's "moat" framing is well-established by the mid-1990s and reaches its definitive form in the 2008 letter, where "widening the moats around our operating businesses" is listed as one of Berkshire's primary jobs. GEICO is the standing example.

> "In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats.' Thanks to Tony [Nicely] and his management team, GEICO's moat widened in 1995."
> — 1995 letter

> "Nonetheless, the economies of scale we enjoy should allow us to maintain or even widen the protective moat surrounding our economic castle."
> — 1996 letter (GEICO)

> "[Our second job is] widening the 'moats' around our operating businesses that give them durable competitive advantages."
> — 2008 letter

> "The company's low costs create a moat – an enduring one – that competitors are unable to cross."
> — 2015 letter (GEICO)

### 2.2 Franchise vs. commodity business

The 1991 letter introduces the franchise-vs-commodity distinction; the 1993 letter elaborates with brands like Coke and Gillette as the prime examples.

> "What we mean by [franchise] is a product or service that (1) is needed or desired, (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute, and (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."
> — 1991 letter

> "Their distribution systems give them an enormous competitive advantage, setting up a protective moat around their economic castles. The average company, in contrast, does battle daily without any such means of protection. As Peter Lynch says, stocks of companies selling commodity-like products should come with a warning label: 'Competition may prove hazardous to human wealth.'"
> — 1993 letter

### 2.3 Pricing power & inflation pass-through

A long-running theme tied to franchises. See's Candies is the recurring exhibit.

> "Inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host organism."
> — 1981 letter (paraphrased; see file for context)

> "The most important quality for an investor is temperament, not intellect."
> — see 1986 letter and reiterations

(Pricing power is illustrated in the See's, GEICO, BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy sections below.)

### 2.4 Capital-light vs. capital-intensive businesses

In the 1980s Buffett extols capital-light "great" businesses (See's, Buffalo News, Cap Cities). After 2009 the rhetoric shifts: BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy are deliberately heavy users of capital, but Buffett defends them as places to deploy Berkshire's cash productively at regulated returns.

> "The best businesses by far for owners continue to be those that have high returns on capital and that require little incremental investment to grow."
> — 2007 letter (paraphrased; see See's discussion)

> "Both BNSF and BHE have two characteristics that differentiate them from other businesses we own. First, both earn good returns on the enormous sums they have invested. Second, both operate in essential fields."
> — 2014 letter

### 2.5 The institutional imperative

Coined in the 1989 letter and revisited in 2004 as a force that pushes managers to keep doing what their peers are doing even when economics turn against them.

> "Most American businesses harbor an 'institutional imperative' that rejects extended decreases in volume. What CEO wants to report to his shareholders that not only did business contract last year but that it will continue to drop?"
> — 2004 letter

(The original 1989 framing is in the "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years" section.)

## Section 3 — Capital allocation

### 3.1 Acquisition criteria

The famous bulleted list ("(1) large purchases…") first appears in the 1983 letter and is restated nearly verbatim every year through 2014. The criteria emphasize size, demonstrated earning power, good ROE without leverage, in-place management, simplicity, and a stated price.

> "Our advertisers tell us that repetition is a key to results (which it is), so we will again repeat our acquisition criteria. We prefer:
> (1) large purchases (at least $5 million of after-tax earnings),
> (2) demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us, nor are 'turn-around' situations),
> (3) businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt,
> (4) management in place (we can't supply it),
> (5) simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it),
> (6) an offering price (we don't want to waste our time or that of the seller by talking, even preliminarily, about a transaction when price is unknown)."
> — 1983 letter (restated annually thereafter; the $5M threshold rises over time)

### 3.2 Share buyback policy & history

Buffett's stance evolved from cool indifference to active programs once Berkshire's cash exceeded what it could deploy. The first explicit repurchase policy was announced in **September 2011** at up to 110% of book value; in 2018 the policy was loosened so that buybacks can occur whenever the board "concurs that … the price is below Berkshire's intrinsic value, conservatively determined."

> "Last September, we announced that Berkshire would repurchase its shares at a price of up to 110% of book value. We were in the market for only a few days – buying $67 million of stock – before the price advanced beyond our limit."
> — 2011 letter

> "It is likely that – over time – Berkshire will be a significant repurchaser of its shares, transactions that will take place at prices above book value but below our estimate of intrinsic value."
> — 2018 letter

> "Obviously, repurchases should be price-sensitive: Blindly buying an overpriced stock is value-destructive, a fact lost on many promotional or ever-optimistic CEOs."
> — 2018 letter

### 3.3 Dividend policy

The 1984 letter contains Buffett's classic exposition of "restricted vs. unrestricted earnings" and the test he applies to retention: every dollar retained should produce at least a dollar of market value. Berkshire has paid a single $0.10 dividend (1967) — repeatedly joked about as a moment when Buffett "must have been in the bathroom."

> "The first test of management is the rationality of capital allocation. … Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect — backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of the future — that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners."
> — 1984 letter

### 3.4 Retained earnings & the power of compounding

A consistent theme from the 1979 letter onward; the framing matures in 2019's "American Tailwind" essay and the 2022 letter, which recasts the entire Berkshire story as the compounding of retained earnings on a few outstanding decisions.

> "Thus began our journey to 2023, a bumpy road involving a combination of continuous savings by our owners (that is, by their retaining earnings), the power of compounding, our avoidance of major mistakes and – most important of all – the American Tailwind."
> — 2022 letter

### 3.5 Stock options as expenses

The 1992–1998 letters contain Buffett's running broadside against the accounting fiction that options "cost nothing." He pushed for, and welcomed, the eventual accounting rule change.

> "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?"
> — 1992 letter (recurring rhetorical structure)

### 3.6 EBITDA critique

Buffett is dismissive of "adjusted EBITDA" presentations across many years; the 2018 and 2024 letters contain the sharpest formulations.

> "That brand of earnings is a far cry from that frequently touted by Wall Street bankers and corporate CEOs. Too often, their presentations feature 'adjusted EBITDA,' a measure that redefines 'earnings' to exclude a variety of all-too-real costs."
> — 2018 letter

> "EBITDA, a flawed favorite of Wall Street, is not for us."
> — 2024 letter

## Section 4 — Insurance & float

### 4.1 The float concept

The single most important conceptual export from the letters: "float is money we hold but don't own." The most lucid statement is in the 1997 letter, but float discussions begin much earlier (1967 first National Indemnity purchase).

> "To begin with, float is money we hold but don't own. In an insurance operation, float arises because premiums are received before losses are paid, an interval that sometimes extends over many years. During that time, the insurer invests the money. … An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds."
> — 1997 letter

> "What should the measure of an insurer's profitability be? Analysts and managers customarily look to the combined ratio … We believe a better measure, however, to be a comparison of underwriting loss to float developed."
> — 1990 letter

### 4.2 GEICO — partial stake to full ownership

Berkshire bought its first GEICO position in 1976 (mentioned in the 1980 letter); the 1995 letter announces the move to 100% ownership. GEICO remains the most-cited operating business in the letters, with the 2024 letter noting Todd Combs's recent overhaul.

> "Common stock was purchased in 1976, 1979 and 1980 at an average of $6.67 per share, and after-tax operating earnings per share last year were $9.01. In cases such as these, Mr. Market has proven to be a mighty good friend."
> — 1987 letter

> "(We ended 2015 with 11.4% of the market compared to 2.5% in 1995, when Berkshire acquired control of GEICO.)"
> — 2015 letter

> "In five years, Todd Combs has reshaped GEICO in a major way, increasing efficiency and bringing underwriting practices up to date. GEICO was a long-held gem that needed major repolishing, and Todd has worked tirelessly in getting the job done."
> — 2024 letter

### 4.3 National Indemnity & super-cat reinsurance

The original insurance operation acquired in 1967. Ajit Jain's super-cat reinsurance operation, built up from 1986, becomes a recurring star of the letters from the 1990s onward.

> "Ajit Jain runs our reinsurance operation from a small office in Stamford, with a staff of 29. They write some of the largest and most complex risks in the world."
> — 2001 letter (paraphrased; see file)

### 4.4 General Re acquisition (1998) and aftermath

The General Re acquisition, completed late 1998, is at first celebrated, then becomes the source of the 2001 9/11 loss and years of underwriting cleanup.

> "We entered 1999 with the best collection of businesses and managers in our history. The two companies we acquired in 1998, General Re and Executive Jet, are first-class in every way."
> — 1998 letter

> "Of more importance, I allowed General Re to take on business without a safeguard I knew was important, and on September 11th, this error caught up with us."
> — 2001 letter

### 4.5 Ajit Jain

Praised by Buffett as one of the most consequential single hires in Berkshire history. In 2018 promoted to oversee all insurance operations.

> "It concerns the management changes we made in early 2018, when Ajit Jain was put in charge of all insurance activities and Greg Abel was given authority over all other operations. These moves were overdue."
> — 2018 letter

### 4.6 Major loss events

Hurricane Andrew (1992), the 9/11 terrorist attacks (2001), the Katrina/Rita/Wilma sequence (2005), and the COVID-19 pandemic (2020) each receive substantial treatment.

> "On September 11th, this error caught up with us. … The tender conditions [for XTRA] included the usual 'out,' allowing us to withdraw if the stock market were to close before the offer's expiration. Throughout much of the 11th, Lew [Rudin] went through a particularly wrenching experience: First, he had a son-in-law working in the World Trade Center who couldn't be [reached]."
> — 2001 letter

> "NFM now owns the three largest home-furnishings stores in the U.S. Each set a sales record in 2020, a feat achieved despite the closing of NFM's stores for more than six weeks because of COVID-19."
> — 2020 letter

## Section 5 — Major holdings & acquisitions

(Year ranges indicate when the position or business is most prominently discussed.)

### 5.1 See's Candies (acquired 1972, discussed 1977–present)

The archetype of a high-return capital-light franchise. Used repeatedly as the lens through which Buffett explains brand value and pricing power.

> "Few businesses we own equal or surpass See's in characteristics we find attractive: predictable revenues and earnings, strong brand identity, and modest capital requirements."
> — (paraphrased framing recurring 1980s–1990s; see for example the 1991 and 1996 letters)

### 5.2 Buffalo News (acquired 1977)

Used in the 1980s letters to teach the economics of "first-newspaper-in-a-one-paper-town" — an example of an "economic franchise" with pricing power before the internet eroded it.

### 5.3 Nebraska Furniture Mart (acquired 1983) and the Blumkin family

Buffett uses Rose Blumkin ("Mrs. B") as the model of the ideal Berkshire manager. She features prominently from the 1983 letter through her death in 1998.

> "When Mrs. B's large family gathered for holiday meals, she always asked that they sing a song before eating. Her selection never varied: Irving Berlin's 'God Bless America.'"
> — 2020 letter

### 5.4 Scott Fetzer (acquired 1985)

Acquisition arrived "the morning mail" with no investment banker. Buffett uses Scott Fetzer for years afterward to illustrate purchase-accounting versus economic earnings.

> "Later sections of this report discuss (a) our purchase of a major position in Capital Cities/ABC, (b) our acquisition of Scott & Fetzer …"
> — 1985 letter (opening)

### 5.5 Capital Cities/ABC (1985–1995) and Tom Murphy

A 1985 marquee equity investment. Murphy becomes one of Buffett's most cited managerial role models; the 2024 letter quotes him explicitly ("praise by name, criticize by category").

### 5.6 Coca-Cola (begun 1988)

The defining 1988–1989 equity buy. Featured in the "Secret Sauce" reflection of the 2022 letter, when the position's value grew with dividends compounding for over three decades.

> "In 1988 we made major purchases of Federal Home Loan Mortgage Pfd. ('Freddie Mac') and Coca Cola. We expect to hold these securities for a long time."
> — 1988 letter

> "In August 1994 – yes, 1994 – Berkshire completed its seven-year purchase of the 400 million shares of Coca-Cola we now own. The total cost was $1.3 billion – then a very meaningful sum at Berkshire."
> — 2022 letter

### 5.7 Wells Fargo (1990 — purchase year)

The 1990 letter contains an extended defense of buying into a bank during a banking-fear cycle. Wells Fargo remained a top holding for nearly three decades before Berkshire exited around 2020–2022.

> "Because leverage of 20:1 magnifies the effects of managerial strengths and weaknesses, we have no interest in purchasing shares of a poorly-managed bank at a 'cheap' price. Instead, our only interest is in buying into well-managed banks at fair prices."
> — 1990 letter

### 5.8 American Express

A long-running holding (Salad Oil crisis purchases in the 1960s, then rebuilt and held). Still listed in the marquee equities through 2024.

> "In the other hand, we own a small percentage of a dozen or so very large and highly profitable businesses with household names such as Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola and Moody's."
> — 2024 letter

### 5.9 Washington Post / Katharine Graham

Bought in 1973, exited around 2014. The Washington Post is also Buffett's recurring stalking-horse for the "beta makes a fallen stock more risky" critique.

> "Under beta-based theory, a stock that has dropped very sharply compared to the market — as had Washington Post when we bought it in 1973 — becomes 'riskier' at the lower price than it was at the higher price."
> — 1993 letter

### 5.10 Dexter Shoe (acquired 1993) — the canonical mistake

Used by Buffett to teach two lessons: misjudging durable competitive advantage, and the compounding cost of paying in Berkshire stock.

> "What I had assessed as durable competitive advantage vanished within a few years. But that's just the beginning: By using Berkshire stock, I compounded this error hugely. That move made the cost to Berkshire shareholders not $400 million, but …"
> — 2007 letter

### 5.11 General Re (acquired 1998)

Initially celebrated, then a multi-year underwriting problem culminating in the 9/11 loss.

> "We entered 1999 with the best collection of businesses and managers in our history. The two companies we acquired in 1998, General Re and Executive Jet, are first-class in every way."
> — 1998 letter

### 5.12 NetJets (Executive Jet, acquired 1998) and FlightSafety (acquired 1996)

NetJets struggled financially during the 2008–2009 crisis ("the major problem for Berkshire last year was NetJets" — 2009 letter) before turning around under David Sokol and successors.

> "The major problem for Berkshire last year was NetJets, an aviation operation that offers fractional ownership of jets. Over the years, it has been enormously successful in establishing itself as the premier company in its industry."
> — 2009 letter

### 5.13 Iscar (acquired 80% in 2006, balance in 2013)

Berkshire's first major international acquisition, in Israel. Used by Buffett to demonstrate Berkshire's willingness to operate globally and pay for excellent management.

> "Nothing stops Israel-based Iscar – not wars, recessions or competitors. The world's two other leading suppliers of small cutting tools both had very difficult years, each operating at a loss throughout much of the year. Though Iscar's results were down significantly from 2008, the company regularly reported profits …"
> — 2009 letter

### 5.14 Marmon (acquired 2008–2013, in stages from Pritzker family)

A massive industrial conglomerate brought in over years.

> "Both Marmon and Iscar turned in relatively strong performances. Frank Ptak's Marmon delivered a 13.5% pre-tax profit margin, a record high."
> — 2009 letter

### 5.15 BNSF Railway (acquired 2010)

The "all-in wager on America's future," announced late 2009 and closed in 2010. Becomes one of the two "towering redwoods" of Berkshire's non-insurance operating side, alongside Berkshire Hathaway Energy.

> "Berkshire's recent acquisition of Burlington Northern Santa Fe (BNSF) has added at least 65,000 shareholders to the 500,000 or so already on our books."
> — 2009 letter

> "Both BNSF and BHE have two characteristics that differentiate them from other businesses we own. First, both earn good returns on the enormous sums they have invested. Second, both operate in essential fields."
> — 2014 letter

### 5.16 Berkshire Hathaway Energy (MidAmerican → BHE)

Bought in stages from 1999 (MidAmerican) onward, including PacifiCorp (2006), NV Energy (2013), and others. Greg Abel ran BHE before being elevated to oversee all non-insurance operations.

> "Our two towering redwoods in this grove are BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (90.9% owned). Combined, they earned $9.3 billion before tax last year."
> — 2018 letter

### 5.17 Heinz / Kraft Heinz (2013 partnership with 3G)

Buffett describes the 3G partnership template enthusiastically in 2013; impairment of Kraft Heinz contributes to a 2018 loss; later letters are more measured.

> "We completed two large acquisitions, spending almost $18 billion to purchase all of NV Energy and a major interest in H. J. Heinz. … With the Heinz purchase, moreover, we created a partnership template that may be used by Berkshire in future acquisitions of size. Here, we teamed up with investors at 3G Capital, a firm led by my friend, Jorge Paulo Lemann."
> — 2013 letter

### 5.18 Lubrizol (announced 2011)

Acquired for ~$9 billion; notable in the letters as a high-quality specialty-chemical platform.

### 5.19 Precision Castparts (acquired 2016)

Cited later (in the 2020 letter) as a writedown — Buffett conceded he paid too much, acknowledging the error candidly.

> "Our next five non-insurance subsidiaries, as ranked by earnings (but presented here alphabetically), Clayton Homes, International Metalworking, Lubrizol, Marmon and Precision Castparts, had aggregate pre-tax income in 2018 of $6.4 billion."
> — 2018 letter

### 5.20 Apple (begun 2016)

Apple debuts in the top-of-portfolio table in the 2017 letter and quickly becomes by far Berkshire's largest equity holding by market value. Buffett refers to Apple as a "third business" alongside the wholly-owned operating companies and insurance, rather than as a tech stock.

> "Apple Inc. … 3.3% [ownership] … cost $20,961 [million], market $28,213 [million]"
> — 2017 letter, equity table

> "Apple … 5.4% … $36,044 … $40,271 [million market value at year-end 2018]"
> — 2018 letter

> "[We own a small percentage of] very large and highly profitable businesses with household names such as Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola and Moody's."
> — 2024 letter

### 5.21 Japanese trading houses (begun 2019, disclosed 2020)

Berkshire built stakes of around 5% each in Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsui, Mitsubishi, and Sumitomo. Buffett discusses them annually thereafter as a position that can compound for decades, financed partly with yen-denominated debt.

> "Itochu Corporation … 5.1% … 1,862 [cost] … 2,336 [market]"
> — 2020 letter, equity table (and similar entries for the other four houses)

### 5.22 Clayton Homes (acquired 2003)

A manufactured-housing platform. Notable for Buffett's contrast between Clayton's prudent lending practices and the 2007–2008 industry-wide subprime collapse.

### 5.23 PetroChina (held 2002–2007)

A short-duration but profitable position bought based on reading the annual report.

> "I bought all of our PetroChina shares for $488 million and sold them in 2007 for $4 billion."
> — 2007 letter (paraphrased; see file)

## Section 6 — Macro & era commentary

### 6.1 Inflation (1970s–1980s; revived 2021–2022)

The 1979–1983 letters are saturated with inflation commentary. Buffett's framework: own businesses with pricing power and low ongoing capital needs. The theme returns in the 2022 letter against the post-pandemic inflation backdrop.

> "We have, by design or accident, purchased only businesses that are particularly well adapted to an inflationary environment. Such favored business must have two characteristics: (1) an ability to increase prices rather easily … and (2) an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases in business … with only minor additional investment of capital."
> — 1981 letter

### 6.2 Corporate tax policy & 2017 tax reform

The 2017 letter is dominated by the windfall to Berkshire from the corporate tax-rate reduction (≈$29 billion of Berkshire's $65 billion gain that year).

> "The $65 billion gain is nonetheless real – rest assured of that. But only $36 billion came from Berkshire's operations. The remaining $29 billion was delivered to us in December when Congress rewrote the U.S. Tax Code."
> — 2017 letter

### 6.3 Derivatives — "weapons of mass destruction"

Buffett's most famous one-liner on financial complexity comes from the 2002 letter, written in the wake of unwinding General Re's derivatives book.

> "In our view, however, derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal."
> — 2002 letter

### 6.4 Trade deficits and the dollar

The 2003 letter is most explicit; Berkshire begins holding foreign-currency contracts as a hedge against dollar weakness.

> "Our trade deficit has greatly worsened … We are like a wealthy family that spends a little more than it earns. As time passes, the family finds itself going through a less and less pleasant succession of activities."
> — 2003 letter (the "Squanderville and Thriftville" parable)

### 6.5 The 1999 tech bubble and Berkshire's worst-relative year

In the 1999 letter Buffett warns about overvalued equities and Berkshire's stock declines in absolute terms even as the Nasdaq surges. The 2001 letter follows the bust.

### 6.6 The 2008 financial crisis and "Buy American"

Buffett's October 2008 New York Times op-ed urging "Buy American. I am." is referenced as backdrop for Berkshire's $14.5 billion of investments in Wrigley, Goldman Sachs, and General Electric preferreds.

> "On the plus side last year, we made purchases totaling $14.5 billion in fixed-income securities issued by Wrigley, Goldman Sachs and General Electric. We very much like these commitments, which carry high current yields that, in themselves, make the investments more than satisfactory."
> — 2008 letter

### 6.7 COVID-19 (2020 letter)

A relatively understated treatment given the scale of the event. Buffett emphasizes resilience of underlying operations and discusses operating closures and recoveries, alongside the gold-standard return-to-normal symbol of the Furniture Mart's record year.

### 6.8 The American Tailwind

Buffett's signature optimistic essay, in the 2018 letter. Reprised in 2019 and 2022.

> "Charlie and I happily acknowledge that much of Berkshire's success has simply been a product of what I think should be called The American Tailwind. … We are lucky – gloriously lucky – to have that force at our back."
> — 2018 letter

> "America would have done fine without Berkshire. The reverse is not true."
> — 2022 letter

## Section 7 — Governance, culture, & disclosure

### 7.1 The Owner's Manual

A standing "owner-related business principles" document, originally written in June 1996 around the Class B share issuance and reprinted (pages-numbered) in each subsequent letter through the 2010s. Defines intrinsic value, lays out Berkshire's prudence on debt, and commits Buffett & Munger to "eat our own cooking."

> "For more explanation of the term, intrinsic value, you may wish to refer to our Owner's Manual, reprinted on pages 62 to 71. This manual sets forth our owner-related business principles."
> — 1997 letter

> "In each annual report, consequently, we restate the economic principles that guide us. … Berkshire has adhered to these principles for decades and will continue to do so long after I'm gone."
> — 2009 letter

### 7.2 Board independence & compensation

Buffett's view: an "independent" director is one whose net worth depends materially on Berkshire stock; he is sharply critical of director-compensation schemes that produce passive enablement of CEOs. Treated repeatedly from the 2002 letter onward.

### 7.3 GAAP critique & operating earnings emphasis

Beginning in 2017 (when ASU 2016-01 forced unrealized equity-portfolio swings through net income), Buffett tells shareholders to focus on operating earnings and to disregard mark-to-market gyrations.

> "Charlie and I urge you to focus on operating earnings – which were little changed in 2019 – and to ignore both quarterly and annual gains or losses from investments."
> — 2019 letter

> "Meanwhile, in what we might call the real world, as opposed to accounting-land, Berkshire's equity holdings averaged about $200 billion during the two years, and the intrinsic value of the stocks we own grew steadily and substantially throughout the period."
> — 2019 letter

### 7.4 Executive compensation and "praise by name, criticize by category"

Buffett refuses to take stock options, restricted stock, or comp tied to short-term results; the 2024 letter quotes Tom Murphy's "praise by name, criticize by category" rule as Berkshire's policy on managing subsidiaries publicly.

> "When discussing problems at specific subsidiaries, we do, however, try to follow the advice Tom Murphy gave to me 60 years ago: 'praise by name, criticize by category.'"
> — 2024 letter

### 7.5 The Berkshire annual meeting and Q&A culture

A recurring closing section in every modern letter. Buffett describes the meeting as "Woodstock for Capitalists" and uses it to test Berkshire managers' presentation and reinforce shareholder culture.

## Section 8 — People & succession

### 8.1 Charlie Munger as partner

Munger is referenced in every letter. The defining tributes appear in the 2014 50th-anniversary essay and the 2023 letter's opening eulogy.

> "Charlie Munger died on November 28, just 33 days before his 100th birthday. … In reality, Charlie was the 'architect' of the present Berkshire, and I acted as the 'general contractor' …"
> — 2023 letter (opening)

> "Charlie, in 1965, promptly advised me: 'Warren, forget about ever buying another company like Berkshire. But now that you control Berkshire, add to it wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices and give up buying fair businesses at wonderful prices. In other words, abandon everything you learned from your hero, Ben Graham. It works but only when practiced at small scale.' With much back-sliding I subsequently followed his instructions."
> — 2023 letter

### 8.2 Munger's "Vice Chairman's Thoughts" (2014, 50th-anniversary essay)

A unique, self-contained Munger essay attached to the 2014 letter that lays out the management system and policies he believed produced Berkshire's success.

> "I closely watched the 50-year history of Berkshire's uncommon success under Warren Buffett. And it now seems appropriate that I independently supplement whatever celebratory comment comes from him."
> — 2014 letter, "Vice Chairman's Thoughts — Past and Future"

### 8.3 Greg Abel — successor CEO

Identified in 2018 as the man overseeing all non-insurance operations; the 2024 letter contains the explicit succession statement.

> "It concerns the management changes we made in early 2018, when Ajit Jain was put in charge of all insurance activities and Greg Abel was given authority over all other operations."
> — 2018 letter

> "At 94, it won't be long before Greg Abel replaces me as CEO and will be writing the annual letters. Greg shares the Berkshire creed that a 'report' is what a Berkshire CEO annually owes to owners."
> — 2024 letter

### 8.4 Ajit Jain — insurance side

The most consequential single hire in Berkshire's modern history, building National Indemnity's super-cat operation from scratch.

> "Think GEICO as a business decision, Ajit Jain as a managerial decision and my luck in finding Charlie Munger as a one-of-a-kind partner, personal advisor and steadfast friend."
> — 2024 letter

### 8.5 Todd Combs & Ted Weschler — investment managers

Hired progressively (Combs in 2010, Weschler in 2012). The 2021 letter quantifies their portfolio responsibility; the 2024 letter credits Combs with reshaping GEICO as CEO.

> "Several of which are selections of Berkshire's two long-time investment managers, Todd Combs and Ted Weschler. At yearend, this valued pair had total authority in respect to $34 billion of investments."
> — 2021 letter

> "In five years, Todd Combs has reshaped GEICO in a major way."
> — 2024 letter

### 8.6 Lou Simpson — GEICO's investment chief (decades-long tenure)

Long-time investment manager at GEICO; periodically praised in the letters as the man Buffett would have brought to Berkshire if Munger weren't already in the seat.

### 8.7 Howard Buffett as future non-executive Chairman

Buffett notes from the 2010s onward that his son Howard is to serve as non-executive Chairman after his passing, charged with cultural continuity and the ability to remove a CEO if needed — not with operating decisions.

### 8.8 The 2024 farewell-as-CEO letter

Buffett's final letter as CEO opens with mistakes acknowledged and explicitly hands the pen to Greg Abel for the future.

> "This letter comes to you as part of Berkshire's annual report. As a public company, we are required to periodically tell you many specific facts and figures. 'Report,' however, implies a greater responsibility."
> — 2024 letter (opening)

> "At 94, it won't be long before Greg Abel replaces me as CEO and will be writing the annual letters."
> — 2024 letter
